# Pola Hujan

## The problem

Indonesians learn that the country has two seasons, rainy and dry. That's true for Java and wrong in much of the rest of the country. Ambon's wet season falls in Java's dry months, and parts of the equatorial belt get two wet peaks a year. BMKG, the national weather agency, knows this. It maps 699 season zones in three families: Monsunal, Ekuatorial and Lokal. But that knowledge lives in PDF bulletins and expert judgement, not in something a curious person can open and explore.

I built Pola Hujan for general Indonesian readers who want to know why "rainy season" means different things in different places. It describes long-term climate. It isn't a forecast or a planting calendar, and the site says so on its first screen.

## What I built

### I computed the map from raw data

The easy route was to digitise BMKG's zone map and colour it in. I computed my own classification from satellite rainfall instead. Each place's twelve-month rainfall cycle is treated as a wave and split into a yearly part and a twice-a-year part, the same maths used to describe tides. Which part is stronger, and when it peaks, decides the family. Anyone can check the result.

### Disagreements with BMKG stay on the map

The thresholds live in one file, each cited to Aldrian and Susanto (2003), the paper behind the three-family system. I set a rule never to adjust them until the map matches BMKG. Two places where my result differs, Medan and Palu, are shown as findings. A separate layer marks every place where the two maps disagree, with sources.

### I tested the classifier on made-up data first

A generator builds rainfall cycles from known wave settings, and the classifier has to recover those exact settings. One permanent test covers a cycle that peaks six months after Java's, which must come out as the inverted family. Only after those tests passed did I run it on satellite data.

### A static site with no heavy libraries

The pipeline downloads CHIRPS satellite archives and reads the raster files with decoders I wrote by hand. There is no mapping, charting, statistics or machine-learning library. Everything runs once at build time, so the site is static, makes no network calls and costs nothing to host.

### The method page shows its work

The method page lists the dataset citation, the exact thresholds, an agreement rate with BMKG calculated by the pipeline, a CSV of the full dataset, and the limits. The main limit: the regional CHIRPS archive stopped updating in October 2016, so this is a ten-year climate record (2006 to 2015), shorter than the 30-year standard.

## Result

Pola Hujan is live, in Indonesian. It has four parts: the atlas, a Jakarta and Ambon comparison, the method page, and an explainer where sliders morph a rainfall cycle between the three families. It covers 34 locations. I checked 14 of the 34 BMKG labels against the agency's own 139-page bulletin, with page numbers, and found and fixed one wrong label (Ternate). The other 20 are marked as estimates. After the build I did a separate accessibility and plain-language pass. I built it in three days in August 2026.

## Under the hood

- The harmonic fit decomposes each location's monthly climatology into annual and semi-annual components. Amplitude ratio and peak phase decide Monsunal, Ekuatorial or Lokal.
- Thresholds live in a single cited config file. Agreement with BMKG is computed by the pipeline, never written by hand.
- A synthetic generator produces cycles from known parameters, with a permanent regression case for the phase-inverted family.
- Hand-written decoders read CHIRPS BIL rasters and tar archives, so there's no GDAL. Download cost is dominated by the 120 monthly rasters, which is why coverage grew from 15 to 34 points at almost no extra cost.
- Fetch, fit and classification run at build time. The output is a static export on GitHub Pages.
- 54 tests across 7 files gate CI. About 3,140 lines of TypeScript, 64 commits over three days.
