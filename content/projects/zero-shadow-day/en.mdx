# Zero Shadow Day

## The problem

Indonesia straddles the equator, so every place in the country gets two days a year when the sun passes straight overhead at noon. On those "zero shadow days", a vertical stick casts almost no shadow. BMKG, the national weather agency, publishes the dates every year, city by city. Almost nobody outside specialists knows it happens, why, or how to show it.

I built Zero Shadow Day for three groups. Residents can find their own city's two dates and see why they fall where they do. Teachers and students get a classroom activity: pair with a school a few hundred kilometres away and repeat Eratosthenes' 2,200-year-old measurement of the Earth. Anyone else gets quick facts on solar noon, sun height and shadow direction.

## What I built

### Everything comes from latitude, longitude and date

The app has no data feed, no API key, no database and no backend. Earlier projects of mine had stalled on data licensing, and a project with no data dependency has nothing to license and nothing to go stale. I wrote the sun-position engine, the shadow geometry, the date search, the Eratosthenes module and the 3D scene myself. There is no astronomy, date, timezone or 3D library.

### I checked the maths before building any screen

The first milestone had no interface at all, only the sun-position maths checked against published astronomical tables. A common shortcut formula for the sun's position can be off by half a degree. Near the tropics, that turns into a date several days wrong that still looks plausible. I also wrote the shadow geometry with no shared code from the sun engine, and a test enforces that. An error then shows up as two calculations disagreeing.

### A shadow never quite vanishes, so I report a window

The sun's disc is about half a degree wide, so a shadow shrinks to a minimum and never reaches nothing. The app always gives a time window, sized from the sun's apparent width on that date. A curious user with a stick outside could disprove a claim of one exact second. To find the dates, I search each year's candidate days for the shortest noon shadow. Near the tropics, where the two dates merge into one at the solstice, this works with no special case.

### Where BMKG and I disagree, the tests say why

I checked the app against 76 dates from BMKG's official tables for 2025 and 2026, covering 38 provincial capitals. Seventy-five match exactly, all 76 fall within a day, and times agree to under five seconds. The one miss is Pekanbaru 2026: I name 22 March, BMKG names the 21st. The city is 0.53° from the equator, so the sun crosses overhead between two noons. Four Papua rows in BMKG's 2025 table appear to list only their first time. A test derives that from the physics.

### The shadow is the readout

The main view is an animated 3D stick and shadow for any place and date. The shadow's length and direction are the numbers, not a picture beside them. I built the 3D scene by hand to keep the whole app at about 110 KB of JavaScript, under a 150 KB budget. A general 3D engine would have used the whole budget on its own.

## Result

Zero Shadow Day is live in Indonesian and English as a static site. Your coordinates stay in the browser, and the app makes no network requests. It shows your two dates with the sun's highest moment to the second, and explains the gap between clock noon and sun noon. It draws shadow-tip curves and the figure-eight analemma. Eratosthenes mode finds a measurement partner elsewhere in the archipelago and shows your error against the true circumference, because the gap is the lesson. A sweep animates the overhead sun across the islands through the year. Any view can be shared as a link, and dates export to a calendar file. I built it in two days in August 2026.

## Under the hood

- `lib/solar` is a NOAA/Meeus solar-position algorithm in pure TypeScript with no React, Next, DOM or network imports. It is shared with Falak, and `tests/solar/extractable.test.ts` keeps it extractable.
- `lib/shadow` is independent of `lib/solar`; a test asserts the import is absent. `lib/zsd` finds dates by minimising noon shadow length; Outside the tropics it returns a structured no-result naming the latitude limit. `lib/eratosthenes` has a synthetic round-trip test that recovers a known radius exactly.
- The numeric core uses Julian Day numbers only. `Date` and timezones live in one display module, `lib/clock`, since Indonesia has three zones and no DST.
- Pekanbaru 2026: the app's rule picks the smaller noon-zenith residual, 0.111° on 22 March against 0.284° on 21 March. The four 2025 rows are Jayapura, Nabire, Wamena and Merauke. Their times are checked against two cities in the same zone and table, whose difference matches the change in the Equation of Time.
- The 3D gnomon is a hand-rolled camera and perspective projection of about 450 lines, with the sky driven by solar altitude. No Three.js.
- The PRD's flat "bleached concrete" palette gave way to a day/night sky and a full-day hero animation. Two rules stayed: vermilion only marks the zero-shadow day, and the shadow is the darkest tone on the page.
- Accessibility pass: focus rings, error-to-field association, real headings, keyboard hints for canvas elements, toggle states and slider announcements. Contrast ratios are asserted in tests.
- Performance pass: cached the per-year declination limits, pinned the variable font to the one weight used, and removed a city-data chunk shipped twice per route.
- Next.js 14 static export on GitHub Pages, strict TypeScript, Tailwind, Vitest, pnpm, self-hosted fonts. CI runs `pnpm test:solar` first, then the full suite, typecheck and lint.
- About 9,600 lines of TypeScript, 189 tests written before the UI (the README badge counts 210 at deploy time), 8 routes in 2 locales, 80 commits over 13 and 14 August 2026.
